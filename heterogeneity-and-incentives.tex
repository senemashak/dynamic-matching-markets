\title{Heterogeneity and Incentives in Dynamic Matching Markets:\\
Outside Options, Liquidity, and Competition between Exchanges}
\author{Sinaya Joshi \\ \textit{Stanford University}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We extend the two-type continuous-time dynamic matching model to allow easy-to-match (E) agents to face outside-option pressure: E agents become critical (depart) at rate $\rho \ge 1$ rather than rate 1. This models potential donors or versatile workers who can exit the matching market when attractive alternatives exist elsewhere. We derive closed-form loss functions for all four canonical policies (Greedy, Patient, Type-Aware Greedy, Type-Aware Patient) as functions of $\rho$ and $d_1$ (H-E compatibility density), revealing a fundamental asymmetry: \textbf{Patient-type policies are directly exposed to outside-option pressure while Greedy-type policies are not, at leading order.}

Key results: $L^{\text{Patient}}(d_1,\rho) = 4\rho W(d_1/(8\rho))/d_1$ and $L^{\text{TA-Greedy}}(d_1,\rho) = 2W(d_1/4)/d_1$ (independent of $\rho$), yielding duality $L^{\text{Patient}}(d_1,\rho) = L^{\text{TA-Greedy}}(d_1/(2\rho))$. For TA-Patient, the exponential decay rate becomes $e^{-d_1/(4(1+\rho))}$: stronger outside options compress the exponential advantage. We analyze competition between platforms, showing private exchanges can dominate public monopoly when markets are thick enough. These findings speak to the empirical debate over kidney exchange consolidation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A central question in matching market design is whether to centralize or fragment. In kidney exchange, the US relies on multiple hospital-run consortia and an OPTN clearinghouse; other countries have experimented with single national registries. The economic literature has emphasized the gains from thicker markets: pooling more agents increases match rates, especially for hard-to-match types. Yet many countries choose decentralized private exchanges. What justifies fragmentation?

This paper identifies a new mechanism: when easy-to-match agents (altruistic donors, bridge donors, versatile workers) have outside options that cause them to leave the market, the key design variable is not just pool size but how quickly the market captures value from easy agents before they exit. A platform that can credibly reduce easy agents' effective departure rate (through priority credits, commitment mechanisms, or better match quality) delivers strictly higher welfare for hard-to-match agents. This is a dimension on which private platforms, unconstrained by public procurement rules, may have an advantage.

\paragraph{The key parameter: $\rho$.}
We introduce the E departure rate $\rho \ge 1$ as a central parameter. When $\rho = 1$, E agents have no special outside-option pressure (baseline). When $\rho > 1$, E agents become critical (and potentially depart) faster. The effective E-liquidity is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lambda}
\Lambda_E \;:=\; \frac{(m/2)}{\rho},
\end{equation}
proportional to the steady-state E pool size. As $\rho$ rises, $\Lambda_E$ falls and hard-type welfare degrades.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Setup}

The market has two types: hard-to-match (H) and easy-to-match (E). Both arrive as Poisson processes at rate $m/2$ each. Compatibility is sparse:
\[
p_{HH} = 0, \quad p_{HE} = \frac{d_1}{2m}, \quad p_{EE} = \frac{d_2}{2m}, \quad d_2 > d_1 > 0.
\]

\textbf{Departure rates.} H agents become critical at rate 1. E agents become critical at rate $\rho \ge 1$. The parameter $\rho$ captures outside-option pressure: $\rho = 1$ is the baseline; $\rho > 1$ models E agents who are more likely to leave for alternatives.

\begin{remark}[Note on $\rho$]
In prior work, $\rho$ was used for the arrival-rate ratio between types. Here, $\rho$ denotes the E departure rate. These are distinct parameters; the arrival rates remain equal at $m/2$ each.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Four policies}

We study:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Greedy}: match on arrival, uniform among compatible neighbours.
\item \textbf{TA-Greedy}: match on arrival, H-priority for E agents.
\item \textbf{Patient}: match at criticality, uniform among compatible neighbours.
\item \textbf{TA-Patient}: match at criticality, H-priority for E agents.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pool dynamics and loss}

The pool state is $(H_t, E_t) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2$. Under any Greedy variant, the pool graph is edgeless (no two pooled agents have tested edges). Under any Patient variant, unrevealed edges among pooled agents remain independent.

The long-run loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss-def}
L \;=\; \frac{\E_\pi[\text{H perish rate}] + \E_\pi[\text{E perish rate}]}{m} \;=\; x^\ast(1-\pi_H) + \rho y^\ast(1-\pi_E),
\end{equation}
where $(x^\ast, y^\ast)$ is the mean-field fixed point.

\section{Analysis with Variable $\rho$}
\label{sec:mf}

\subsection{Greedy: leading-order independence from $\rho$}
\label{sec:greedy-rho}

The Greedy drift is:
\begin{align}
F_1(x,y) &= \tfrac12(1-\pi_H) - x - \tfrac12 r_{EH},\\
F_2(x,y) &= \tfrac12(1-\pi_E) - \rho y - \tfrac12 \pi_H - \tfrac12 r_{EE}.
\end{align}

At fixed point, $x + \rho y = 1 - \pi_H - \pi_E$ and $x - \rho y = r_{EE}$.

\begin{lemma}[Greedy loss is $\rho$-independent at leading order]
\label{lem:greedy-rho-indep}
\proved As $d_2 \to \infty$, the limiting $x$-coordinate is the unique root in $(0,1/2)$ of
\[
g_{d_1}(x) := x\,\exp\!\left(\frac{d_1 x(1-x)}{2(1-2x)}\right) = 1,
\]
independent of $\rho$. Hence $L^{\text{Greedy}}(d_1,\rho) = x^\ast(d_1) + O(1/d_2)$, with the leading term independent of $\rho$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Write $\rho y = c/d_2$ and examine the $d_2 \to \infty$ scaling. The $\rho y$ terms vanish relative to $O(1)$ terms. The resulting scalar equation for $x$ does not involve $\rho$, so $x^\ast$ is $\rho$-independent.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Intuition]
Under Greedy, matching happens at agent \emph{arrival}. The E pool serves as standing inventory for H arrivals. While higher $\rho$ shrinks this inventory, at leading order in dense markets ($d_2 \to \infty$), the inventory is large enough regardless. The matching density $d_1$ is the binding constraint.
\end{remark}

\subsection{TA-Greedy: same independence}

By similar analysis: $L^{\text{TA-Greedy}}(d_1,\rho) = 2W(d_1/4)/d_1 + O(1/d_2)$, independent of $\rho$ at leading order.

\subsection{Patient: closed-form $\rho$-dependence}
\label{sec:patient-rho}

\begin{lemma}[Patient loss with $\rho$]
\label{lem:patient-rho}
\proved As $d_2 \to \infty$, the Patient fixed point satisfies $x \to 1/2$ and $y \to y^\ast(d_1,\rho)$ where $e^{-d_1 y^\ast/2} = 4\rho y^\ast$. Via Lambert $W$:
\[
\boxed{L^{\text{Patient}}(d_1,\rho) = \frac{4\rho\, W(d_1/(8\rho))}{d_1}.}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
From fixed-point equations as $d_2 \to \infty$, derive $e^{-d_1 y/2} = 4\rho y$. Setting $u = d_1 y/2$ gives $u e^u = d_1/(8\rho)$, so $u = W(d_1/(8\rho))$ and $y = 2W(d_1/(8\rho))/d_1$. The loss $L = 2\rho y = 4\rho W(d_1/(8\rho))/d_1$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Behavior in $\rho$.}
\proved As $\rho \to 1$, recover the baseline. As $\rho \to \infty$, loss $\to 1/2$ (market collapses). As $\rho \to 0$, loss $\to 0$ (E agents permanently sticky).

The log-elasticity is $\partial \log L / \partial \log \rho = W(d_1/(8\rho))/(1+W(d_1/(8\rho))) \in (0,1)$.

\subsection{TA-Patient: identities and exponential decay}
\label{sec:TAP-rho}

\begin{lemma}[TA-Patient identities]
\label{lem:TAP-identities-rho}
\proved Any solution of the TA-Patient system (as $d_2 \to \infty$) satisfies:
\begin{align}
L^{\text{TA-Patient}} &= x\,e^{-d_1 y/2},\\
x\,e^{-d_1 y/2} &= 2\rho y\,e^{-d_1 x/2}.
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{conjecture}[TA-Patient decay with $\rho$]
\label{conj:TAP-rho}
For $d_1 \to \infty$ at fixed $\rho$:
\[
\boxed{L^{\text{TA-Patient}}(d_1,\rho) \;\sim\; \frac{(2\rho)^{1/(1+\rho)}}{2(1+\rho)}\,e^{-d_1/(4(1+\rho))}.}
\]
\end{conjecture}

The decay exponent $A(\rho) = 1/(4(1+\rho))$ is strictly decreasing in $\rho$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{r|cccccc}
$\rho$ & $0.5$ & $1$ & $2$ & $5$ & $10$ & $20$\\\hline
$A(\rho)$ & $1/6$ & $1/8$ & $1/12$ & $1/24$ & $1/44$ & $1/84$\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Doubling $\rho$ (from 1 to 2) requires $50\%$ more H-E compatibility density to achieve the same loss level.

\section{Duality and Policy Comparisons}
\label{sec:duality}

\begin{lemma}[$\rho$-generalized Patient--TA-Greedy duality]
\label{lem:duality-rho}
\proved
\[
L^{\text{Patient}}(d_1,\rho) \;=\; L^{\text{TA-Greedy}}\!\left(\frac{d_1}{2\rho}\right).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{remark}
Patient matching at outside-option pressure $\rho$ is equivalent to TA-Greedy at H-E density $d_1/(2\rho)$. Increasing $\rho$ by factor $k$ costs as much as reducing H-E density by factor $1/(2k)$.
\end{remark}

\section{Competition Between Exchanges}
\label{sec:competition}

\subsection{Two-platform model}

Platform A (public): uses Patient, all H agents, fraction $\alpha$ of E agents, E departure rate $\rho_0$.

Platform B (private): uses TA-Patient, no H agents (or shared via cross-platform rescue), fraction $1-\alpha$ of E agents, E departure rate $\rho_B < \rho_0$ (reduced via incentives).

The effective E departure rate in the composite system:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eff-rho}
\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}(\alpha) \;=\; \left[\alpha/\rho_0 + (1-\alpha)/\rho_B\right]^{-1}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Welfare comparison}

\begin{proposition}[Competition threshold]
\label{prop:competition}
\open Competition is welfare-improving when:
\[
L^{\text{TA-Patient}}(d_1, \rho_{\mathrm{eff}}) \;<\; L^{\text{Patient}}(d_1, \rho_0).
\]
For large $d_1$, this holds (exponential vs. polynomial decay). For moderate $d_1$, a threshold $d_1^\ast(\rho_0, \rho_B, \alpha)$ determines the regime.
\end{proposition}

\section{Applications}
\label{sec:applications}

\subsection{Kidney exchange}
In kidney exchange, H agents are incompatible patient-donor pairs, E agents are altruistic donors. Outside options for altruistic donors include: donating to friends/family, other exchanges, delaying donation. Our findings suggest:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Public exchanges using Greedy or Patient are not $\rho$-sensitive at leading order, but fail to leverage exponential rescue capacity.
\item Private exchanges using TA-Patient can exponentially outperform public exchanges if they invest in donor retention.
\item The optimal structure depends on market thickness: thin markets favor single pool; thick markets favor competition with better policy and retention.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Gig economy platforms}
In gig platforms, H agents are hard-to-serve jobs, E agents are versatile workers with many outside options ($\rho$ captures churn). Type-aware patience combined with worker retention (credits, stability guarantees) is the right strategy.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Finite-$d_2$ effects.}
O($1/d_2$) corrections in Greedy and TA-Greedy do depend on $\rho$. The crossover $d_2^*$ where policy rankings flip in $\rho$ is an open numerical question.

\paragraph{Formal competition model.}
The two-platform analysis relies on the effective-rate approximation. A rigorous treatment would model the joint CTMC explicitly and derive the coupled mean-field fixed-point equations.

\paragraph{Stationary concentration.}
Transfer from ODE fixed points to CTMC long-run losses requires the stationary distribution to concentrate at the mean-field fixed point. This is supported by numerical evidence and is an extension of prior work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown that the E departure rate $\rho$ introduces a fundamental asymmetry: Patient-type policies are $\rho$-sensitive (direct exposure to outside options) while Greedy-type policies are not (at leading order). The closed form $L^{\text{Patient}}(d_1,\rho) = 4\rho W(d_1/(8\rho))/d_1$ makes this precise and yields the duality $L^{\text{Patient}}(d_1,\rho) = L^{\text{TA-Greedy}}(d_1/(2\rho))$.

For TA-Patient---the exponentially superior policy---higher $\rho$ compresses the decay exponent from $d_1/8$ (at $\rho=1$) to $d_1/(4(1+\rho))$: a platform that retains E agents (reduces $\rho$) gains exponentially in thick markets. This creates a rationale for private exchanges that invest in E-agent retention. The competition model suggests fragmentation is not always harmful: if a private platform sufficiently reduces $\rho$ and uses TA-Patient, welfare gains dominate fragmentation costs for thick markets.
